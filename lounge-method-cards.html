Reach again

What repair works on me, even while I am still cross?

Most decks ask you to be interesting. This one assumes you are already tired.

Every card names the moment you would reach for it, asks you one question, and then shows you what an answer could sound like said out loud.

That last part is the bit nobody prints. Knowing what you feel is not the hard bit. Getting it across the room is.

These cards are for ordinary relationship conflict. They are not a tool for coercive control, intimidation, threats, stalking, sexual pressure or violence. If someone is using them to press you, put them down.

Pass is a whole answer. So is “I cannot find it in my body”, and so is not tonight. Nobody has to earn their way out of a card.

The other fifty five

A stack of questions is not a deck.

Every conversation deck on the market hands you prompts and walks away. Not one of them tells you how to stop, what to do when you cannot speak, or what happens to the card that landed hardest. The full deck does.

Pass, printed on the card

Not buried in the rules where nobody looks. On every single card, where the person who needs it can see it.

Deal me three

They pick three they would like to hear. You read all three and answer one. Their curiosity, your choice.

Say it, write it, or take it away

Not everyone thinks out loud. Write the answer instead, or take the card and bring it back tomorrow.

Two protected minutes

Two minutes where the other person does not speak. For when one of you is quicker with words than the other.

The overlap

You each mark a private list. It shows only what you both said yes to. What one of you wanted alone stays yours.

Ask me again

The deck remembers. A card that was too big tonight can come back in six months and find a different answer.

Enough for tonight

One tap, always on screen. Stopping is not the same as giving up, and it should not take a speech.

Nothing is in play unless you put it there

You choose the suits before you start. No one has to remove a card in front of anybody.

The seventh suit

The stripe the flag lost meant sex.

Gilbert Baker’s original 1978 flag had eight stripes, and hot pink sat at the top of it. Every stripe carried a word. Hot pink’s word was sex. The six stripe flag most of us grew up under does not have it.

So the deck put it back, and gave it a suit. The wanting is twenty cards on desire, refusal, sensory need, mismatch and repair, written for bodies that do not all work the same way and for people who were never handed a script.

It starts switched off. You turn it on when you want it, not the other way round.
