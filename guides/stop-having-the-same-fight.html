We keep having the same fight. How do we stop?

You know the one. It starts in the car on the way home from a family lunch, or over whether you could hold hands at the work party, or over the dishes. Twenty minutes later neither of you is talking about what started it. You are somewhere you have both been before, saying things you have both heard before, and it ends the way it usually ends.

This guide is for two of you, or more than two, who keep landing in the same place. Read it together or on your own. Every question in it can be passed.

About 9 minutesFor two of you, or moreRead together or alone

If that is you, nothing is broken. Most couples have one or two fights they keep coming back to, and many of those never fully resolve. Solving the topic for good is not the goal. What changes things is how you go through the fight, and how you find your way back after it.

If you only do one thing tonight

Next time the fight starts, try this sentence, word for word:

I care about this, and I cannot do it well right now. I need ____ minutes. I will come back at ______. If that time needs to change, I will tell you rather than disappear.

Then come back when you said you would. The rest of this guide explains why it works.

Before you read on

This one lands better when you both read it. Send it to your partner, or partners, and read it on your own first if that feels easier.

The fight is not the topic

The topic changes. The loop does not. In my work I call it the miss: the moment one of you reaches and it does not quite land, and both of you start protecting yourselves.

It often looks like this. One of you moves toward the problem: more words, more questions, a need to sort it now. The other moves away from it: quieter, shorter, a need for space. The more one pursues, the more the other retreats. The more one retreats, the more the other pursues. Neither of you is doing it wrong. You are both doing what has kept you safe.

Something I see all the time: two people who love each other, each certain the other one started it. Most of the time, neither of you started it. The loop did.

Try describing your own loop with verbs, not labels:

  • “I pursue; you go quiet.”
  • “I explain; you defend.”
  • “I fix; you feel managed.”
  • “We both go cold and wait for the other to soften first.”

Name the loop, not the person. The loop is the problem. Naming it does not make responsibility equal, and it does not mean the impact on each of you is the same. Power, fear, freedom, safety and impact still matter. What naming it gives you is something to look at together, instead of at each other.

Not sure what your loop looks like yet? The free Find the Miss quiz names the rhythm you tend to fall into, and what is already working in it.

What each of you is protecting

Underneath the same fight there is usually a quieter question. Most of us are not really arguing about the dishes. We are asking something closer to one of these:

  • Do I still matter to you?
  • Am I carrying this relationship alone?
  • Will there be room for me if I am fully myself?
  • Will closeness cost me my freedom?
  • Can I need you without losing dignity?
  • Can you see the impact even when you did not intend it?

You do not have to answer these out loud. Just notice which one feels closest. Often each of you has a different one, which is part of why the fight makes so little sense from the other side.

The way you protect yourself makes sense from the inside. It can be hard to receive from the outside. Pursuing can land as criticism. Going quiet can land as leaving. Neither is the whole truth about the person doing it.

Catch it early

Every loop has an early signal, a moment just before the old script takes over. It might be a tight chest, a jaw that sets, a sentence that starts with “You always”. It might be the moment one of you picks up a phone.

The earlier you catch it, the more choice you have. It helps to know which of three places you are in:

  • Connected enough: curiosity, nuance, flexible thinking, humour, room for repair.
  • Mobilised protection: pursue, prove, defend, fix, escape.
  • Shutdown protection: fog, numbness, appeasing, going still, losing words.

A settled nervous system is not proof that someone is right. A strong emotional response is not proof that someone is wrong. Both just tell you where you are, and most of the work starts there, before anyone tries to solve anything.

Five moves for the same fight

These are the five moves I use with couples, in the order they usually happen. You will not manage all of them every time. One is enough to change how the night goes.

  1. Notice what arrived.A body cue, the meaning that came with it, and anything else that walked into the room with you, like a hard day or an old story. “My chest is tight, and I am already hearing that I do not matter. Can I tell you what is happening for me?”
  2. Name what took over.Describe the loop, not the person. “I think our loop just started. I am pushing and you are pulling away. Can we look at it together?”
  3. Pause without disappearing.A pause has a return time. Silence used to punish, frighten or control is not a pause.
  4. Find a way back to yourself.Your body, a person, your culture, a place, something that matters to you. Breath is optional. Whatever actually brings you back counts.
  5. Reach again.Your truth, the difference between you, the impact, and one clear request. “I got scared you were done with me. Could we sit together for ten minutes before we talk about it again?”

The bridge back

If you need a pause, this is the sentence. Say it as written the first few times, then make it your own.

I care about this, and I cannot do it well right now. I need ____ minutes. I will come back at ______. If that time needs to change, I will tell you rather than disappear.

Then come back when you said you would. That is what makes a pause trustworthy. When the person who pauses keeps coming back, the person who waits can learn to let them go.

Words that leave room for two realities

When you go back in, you are not trying to win the version of events. You are trying to understand two of them.

  • “What did that moment mean to you? What am I not seeing yet?”
  • “Your experience is different from mine. I want to understand it without giving up my own.”
  • “My intention was… and I can hear that the impact was… Both matter.”
  • “My part in our loop was…”
  • “What I am asking for now is… What would make that possible for you?”

When the fight is bigger than the two of you

For queer couples, the same fight often has more people in it than the ones in the room. A family that still gets a name or a pronoun wrong. A workplace where one of you stays careful. Being out to different people, at different speeds. A culture, a community, a body that has had to work hard to be seen. Years of staying alert in places that were not safe.

None of that is your partner’s fault, and none of it cancels what happened between you. But if you only look at the two of you, you miss part of what is going on. Sometimes the kindest thing either of you can say is, “I think this is bigger than us tonight. Can we be on the same side of it?”

Try this when things are calm

Not in the middle of the fight. Choose a quiet time when you are both connected enough. Each of you answers for yourself first, on your own. The way back does not belong to one “better regulated” partner.

  1. What happened on the surface?
  2. What did my body prepare me for?
  3. What did the moment come to mean?
  4. What was I protecting?
  5. What might my protection have been like to receive?
  6. What wider story entered the room?
  7. What do I want to take responsibility for?
  8. How could I reach again?

Then, together, write down three things:

  • Our early signal: the first sign the loop is starting.
  • Our bridge back: the sentence each of you will use.
  • Our first repair move: one return line, one piece of accountability, one clear request.

Choose one of the three to try the next time the same fight starts, and tell each other which one you chose.

Common questions

We have had this fight for years. Is it too late?

No. A long loop is well practised, not permanent. Couples change old fights all the time, often by changing the first ten minutes rather than the topic.

What if only one of us wants to try this?

Start with your own part. When one person changes their side of the loop, the loop has to change. You could say, “I am going to try something different next time. Would you tell me if you notice?”

Is it bad that we fight?

No. Conflict is not the problem. Fights that never come back to repair are. Difference is not disconnection.

What if one of us goes quiet for days?

Then the pause has lost its return time. Name it gently, and agree on a time to come back. If silence is being used to punish, frighten or control, that is not a pause, and the next section is for you.

Does this work for more than two of us?

Yes. Each person answers for themselves first, and each pair inside the relationship may have its own loop to map.

When is it time for couples counselling?

When the loop is winning more often than you are, or when every attempt to go back in turns into the same fight. That is exactly what sessions are for.

What this guide is not for

This guide is for relationships where everyone is trying. It is not for coercive control, intimidation, threats, stalking, sexual pressure or violence. If you are afraid of your partner, or afraid of what happens when you disagree, that is not a communication problem.
