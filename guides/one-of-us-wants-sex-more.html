When one of you wants sex more often. How do we talk about it?

One of you wants sex more often than the other. Maybe it has always been this way. Maybe it crept in after a move, a new job, a new child in the house, a diagnosis, a change in medication or a change in your body. Either way, one of you has started counting the weeks, and the other has started bracing when the lights go off.

This guide is for two of you, or more than two. Read it on your own first. Every question in it can be passed, and a pass needs no reason. Nobody reads this guide at anybody.

About 10 minutesFor two of you, or moreEvery question can be passed

If that is you, neither of you is the problem. A gap in how often people want sex is one of the most common things couples bring to counselling. It is not a sign the relationship is ending, and it is not a sign that one of you is broken.

If you only do one thing tonight

Make a no safe. If you are the one who wants sex more often, try saying this, outside the bedroom:

You can always say no to me, and I will not make you pay for it. I want your yes only when it is a real one.

If you are the one who says no more often, and it is true for you, try:

When I say no to sex, I am not saying no to you. You still matter to me.

Neither line promises anything else. That is the point.

Before you read on

This one lands better when you both read it. Send it to your partner, or partners, and read it on your own first if that feels easier.

It is not high against low

The usual story is that one person wants a lot and the other wants very little, and the job is to meet in the middle. That story tends to make things worse. It turns one of you into the person who wants too much, and the other into the person who is failing.

More often, you need different things first. For one of you, closeness comes before sex: you need to feel connected to feel sexual. For the other, sex is how closeness happens: you need sex to feel connected. Each of you is waiting for the other to go first, and each of you is right about what you need.

Sex can also mean something different to each of you. Comfort. Reassurance. Excitement. Being chosen. A way out of your own head for a while. When one of you says “we never have sex”, it is worth asking what that sex would have given you.

Wanting arrives in different ways, too. For some people it turns up out of nowhere. For others it arrives once they are already close, touched, relaxed and safe. Some people call this responsive desire. Neither way is more real, and either can belong to anyone, of any gender.

Something I see all the time: the person who wants sex more often feels rejected, and the person who wants it less often feels they are failing. Most of the time, both of them are trying to protect the relationship.

What counts as sex is yours to decide

Queer relationships often do not come with one script for what sex is. For one of you it might mean one particular thing. For the other it might include touch, play, kissing for a long time, or things you do together that nobody ever named. So when one of you says “we never have sex”, the other may be thinking of last Tuesday. Both can be true.

It can help to ask, gently and with no right answer: what counts as sex for me, and what counts for you? Some couples find the gap is smaller than they thought once they compare. Others find it is a different gap, and that one is often easier to talk about than the one they were fighting about.

What usually grows around the gap

When the gap has been there a while, other things grow around it.

  • Fear of rejection. After enough nos, reaching out starts to cost something. The person who wants sex more often starts asking less, or asking sideways, so the no hurts less.
  • Hesitation to start anything. The person who wants sex less often may stop offering even a cuddle, in case it is read as an invitation they cannot follow through on.
  • Going along with it. Some people say yes to sex they do not want, to keep the peace or to quiet the worry. It can look like a solution. Over time it usually makes wanting harder, not easier.
  • The load. When one of you is carrying the household, the appointments, the money worry or the caring, it is hard to feel desire while you also feel responsible for everything. That is a load problem, not a wanting problem.
  • A body that registers stress first. For many neurodivergent people, and for anyone who is exhausted, in pain, or living in a body that has been through a lot, touch can land as stress before it can land as pleasure.

For queer couples there is often more. A body changing with hormones or surgery. Dysphoria that comes and goes. A history of having to hide, or of sex being something other people had loud opinions about. None of that is a problem to fix before sex can work for you. It needs room, and it needs talking about.

The loop it makes

Like most stuck places, this one turns into a loop. One of you reaches, sometimes in words, sometimes with just a hand on a hip. The other pulls back, or goes still, or goes along with it. The one who reached feels unwanted. The one who pulled back feels pressured. Next time the reach is a little more careful and a little more loaded, and the pulling back comes a little sooner.

In my work I call that moment the miss: one of you reaches and it does not quite land. Both of you are protecting something real. One of you may be protecting the hope of being wanted. The other may be protecting the right to say no without it costing the relationship. Neither of those is wrong, and the way out keeps both of them safe.

Five moves for this loop

  1. Notice what arrived.“When you rolled away I felt the old drop in my stomach, and I heard: you do not want me.” Or: “When your hand moved, my shoulders went up before I had decided anything.”
  2. Name what took over.Describe the loop, not the person. “I think I reached, you braced, and now we are both protecting ourselves. Can we look at it together?”
  3. Pause without disappearing.A no to sex is a complete answer and needs no return time. Walking away from the conversation about sex is different. “Not tonight, and I do want to talk about us. Can we talk on Sunday morning?”
  4. Find a way back to yourself.Wanting and not wanting both live in the body. Whatever brings you back to yourself counts. Breath is optional.
  5. Reach again.One true thing and one clear request, said outside the bedroom. “I miss feeling chosen by you. Could we keep Sunday evening just for us, with no expectation about where it goes?”

If the conversation itself gets too much, the bridge back still works:

I care about this, and I cannot do it well right now. I need ____ minutes. I will come back at ______. If that time needs to change, I will tell you rather than disappear.

A few things to try

  • Make a no safe first. A no to sex does not need a reason, and it does not need repairing. Consent is something each of you actively gives, every time, and it can change at any point. When a no costs nothing, a yes means more.
  • Set aside the time, not the outcome. Time together with nothing expected at the end of it takes the pressure off. You are making room, not booking sex.
  • Agree on touch that does not have to lead anywhere. A hug that is just a hug. Some couples agree on a word or a signal that means “this is closeness, not an invitation”, so the person who wants sex less often can lean in without bracing.
  • Talk about it with your clothes on. The hardest time to talk about sex is straight after a no. Choose a calm time, in another room.
  • Aim for good enough. Not every time has to be the best time. Sex that is warm, a bit awkward and good enough often does more for a relationship than waiting for perfect.
  • Notice feeling wanted, not the count. There is no number of times a week that a healthy relationship needs. The better question is whether each of you feels wanted, and free.

Questions to answer on your own

Each of you answers for yourself first. None of these asks you to rate anyone else.

  1. When I want sex, what am I usually hoping it will give me?
  2. What counts as sex for me, and do I know what counts for you?
  3. What makes wanting feel just about within reach for me?
  4. What tends to put it out of reach?
  5. When I hear a no, or say one, what does it come to mean?
  6. What do I want to take responsibility for in our loop?
  7. What could I ask for that has nothing to do with how often?

Then share one answer each, and choose one small thing to try this week.

Common questions

Is there a right amount of sex?

No. There is no number a healthy relationship needs. What matters is whether the arrangement works for each of you, and whether a yes or a no feels free.

Is one of us broken?

No. A gap in wanting is common, and it is not a diagnosis. If something physical has changed, like pain, a new medication or hormones, it is worth talking with your GP as well.

Should the one who wants sex less often just try harder?

No. Going along with sex you do not want tends to make wanting harder over time. What helps is more room, not more pressure.

Should the one who wants sex more often just stop asking?

Also no. Your wanting is not too much. What helps is a way of asking that nobody has to brace against.

What if we are not monogamous?

The gap can still sit between any two people in the relationship. Other connections can take some pressure off, or add some. It is worth talking honestly about which it is, rather than assuming.

What if one of us does not experience much sexual attraction at all?

That is not something to fix, and it does not mean the relationship cannot work. Many people who experience little or no sexual attraction still want closeness, touch, romance, or some kinds of sex and not others. The same things help: room for each of you, and a way of talking that nobody has to brace against.

When is it time for couples counselling?

When the topic has gone quiet because it hurts too much to raise, or when every conversation about it ends in the same place. That is what sessions are for, and talking about sex in the room is ordinary.

What this guide is not for

This guide is for relationships where everyone is trying. It is not for sexual pressure, coercion, or sex that happens because someone is afraid of what will happen if they say no. If you are being pressured, threatened or made to feel afraid, that is not a gap in wanting.
